\begin{abstract}
A number of studies have shown that cumulative mortality followed a Gompertz curve in the
initial Covid pandemic period in March-April 2020 --- a pattern incompatible with the
initial logistic growth predicted by traditional Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR)
models. We ask what the mortality data can actually determine about the two competing
descriptions, and find that the SIR family's reputation for simplicity does not survive
the question. Fitted to deaths alone, the SIRD model carries one parameter more than the
observations support: its scaled Jacobian is conditioned an order of magnitude worse than
that of the Gompertz model ($18$--$594$ against $3.8$--$4.4$ across sixteen countries),
and the ill-conditioned direction is precisely the one carrying the reproduction number
$\mathcal{R}_0$. The consequences are measurable. Changing how an even-length moving
average is centred --- a half-day shift with no modelling content --- moves the fitted
$\mathcal{R}_0$ by a median of $15\%$ while moving the Gompertz rate by $1.4\%$; and two
careful fits of the same equation to the same source data differ by a quarter in
$\mathcal{R}_0$ while agreeing in the median to about one per cent on the combination the
data actually determine. We do not conclude that the SIRD model is false: over a single
wave it fits the observed relationship between deaths and their rate marginally better
than the Gompertz model does, and we argue that a formal test between the two is a weak
instrument at these sample sizes. The conclusion is narrower --- that the quantity these
models are most often fitted in order to report is not identifiable from aggregate
mortality, and that the precision with which it is routinely quoted is an artefact of an
error model which ignores the smoothing applied to the data. When the fitted equations
are instead integrated forward, the Gompertz model tracks the observed trajectory more
closely in eleven of the sixteen countries. We connect this to a mechanistic account in
which Gompertz growth is the unique member of the Richards family closed under
aggregation, so that the transmission network contributes nothing to the collective
curve --- a population from which no transmission parameter could be recovered by
curve-fitting in the first place.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}
Traditional communicable disease spreading theory assumes a pathogen which infects the population through a network of transmission. 
Following this line of reasoning it can be theoretically shown that in the early stages of an epidemic, growth follows a logistic-like curve, where the very beginning exhibits exponential growth, and for which analytic and semi-analytic solutions have recently emerged \cite{harko2014exact,kroger2020analytical,schlickeiser2021analytical,heng2020approximately}. 
A large body of research reveals how these models fit the mortality seen due to the Covid pandemic in 2020, a pandemic due to an influenza-like disease \cite{carletti2020covid,cooper2020sir,postnikov2020estimation,munoz2021sir,cooper2022dynamical,saikia2021covid}. 

However, instead of showing logistic-like growth, observed cumulative mortality in the initial period March-April 2020 exhibits almost perfect resemblance to Gompertzian growth \cite{Gompertz1825,bajzer1997mathematical} where the log-transformed cumulative mortality, or log-mortality for short, is exponentially \emph{decreasing} in time,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:GompertzODEsimple}
\frac{d}{dt}\ln{F(t)} = -\beta\ln{F(t)},
\end{equation} 
using normalized mortality such that $F(t\rightarrow\infty) = 1$.
% \insertSmallPdfFig{Gompertz_concept}{A conceptual display of the discrepancy seen between the logistic growth and the observations, superimposed by a Gompertz curve. Data is transformed to the straight line domain of the Gompertz curve for visual clarity, $f(Y)=\ln{\ln{1/Y}}$.}

This phenomenon is recorded by others \cite{Ohnishi2020,Rypdal2020,Catala2020,rodrigues2020monte,Levitt2020}, showing Gompertz curves at national levels instead of the predicted logistic curves, an observation made possible in part due to the increased cadence and quality of data acquisition compared with earlier epidemics. What is causing such a discrepancy between these observations and the current theory of communicable diseases \cite{castro2020turning}? 

In addition to the characteristic patterns of these national mortality curves, other observations that conflict more generally with the communicable disease models are 1) the lack of correlation between population density and mortality (or infection) rates \cite{Hamidi2020,Hamidi2020a,Carozzi2020,liu2020emerging,medica2020covid,khavarian2021high,barak2021urban}, 2) no clear association between inter-generational relationships and fatality rates \cite{Arpino2020}, and 3) the widespread morbidity and mortality spikes seen during spring 2020 among birds \cite{Fischer2021}, dogs \cite{PeriseBarrios2021}, rabbits \cite{Duff2020,Hu2020,Fukui2021}, elephants \cite{Aarde2021}, and horses \cite{Kambayashi2021,King2020,Lu2020,CastilloOlivares2020}. Many of these animal mortality spikes are not attributable to a Coronavirus infection, so why did these spikes all occur within the same time frame? While these observations are not reconcilable within the current communicable disease paradigm, we present here an alternative theory that can reconcile all observations; a theory based on a global biosphere disturbance with fewer parameters than the most basic communicable disease model. 

This global biosphere disturbance could be in the form of sudden ionospheric disturbances (SIDs) \cite{cannon1982schumann}, which in the context of short-term solar and geomagnetic disturbances have been associated with physiological changes in cardiac system processes \cite{vieira2022geomagnetic,Alabdulgader2018,ghione1998geomagnetic,chernouss2001geophysical,stoupel1995ambulatory,stoupel2011twenty,malin1979correlation,stoupel1993sudden}, melatonin/serotonin balance \cite{burch1999geomagnetic,rapoport1997effects,bergiannaki1996seasonal}, endothelial activation and inflammation \cite{schiff2022role}, and disturbed magnetoreception in migrating animals \cite{keeton1974normal,schreiber1976correlation,kowalski1988normal,bianco2019magnetic,ferrari2017cetacean,vanselow2018solar,granger2020gray}. More general effects have also been studied in this context, e.g. overall mortality, suicide, mental disorders, homicides, and epileptic seizures \cite{zenchenko2021possible,zilli2019geomagnetic,gordon2003effect,kay1994geomagnetic,kay2004schizophrenia,halberg2005chronomics,berk2006ambient,doronin1998effect,persinger1995sudden}. Cherry (2002) put forward a mechanism whereby the ionosphere acts as a coupling agent between geomagnetic disturbances and physiological effects based on the disturbance of the background electromagnetic oscillations between the surface of the Earth and the lower ionosphere, called the Schumann Resonance signal \cite{cherry2002schumann,schumann1952strahlungslosen}. The Schumann Resonance would then couple with the brain to alter the melatonin/serotonin balance, which was a causal link observed by Wever (1974) through the circadian rhythm \cite{wever1974elf}. Indeed, more recent findings support this hypothesized synchronization between Schumann Resonances and the brain \cite{pobachenko2006contingency,saroka2014quantitative,persinger2015human,konig2012biologic}.

In support of Cherry's theory, specific phenomena such as solar X-ray bursts \cite{dyrda2015novel,roldugin2004schumann,satori2005response,satori2016effects,shvets2017effect}, solar proton events \cite{roldugin2004schumann,schlegel1999schumann,singh2014x} and geomagnetic storms \cite{pazos2019analysis,salinas2016solar} can modify the shape of the ionosphere cavity and shift the Schumann resonance frequencies \cite{kudintseva2018modifications,satori2016effects,zhou2015studies}. Seismic activity has also been connected to variations in Schumann resonances \cite{christofilakis2019significant,galuk2019scattering,hayakawa2020scattering}, and conversely, ionospheric modifications have been connected to global seismicity \cite{Marchitelli2020}. Zaporozhan and Ponomarenko (2010) postulated that minute disturbances in magnetic fields which could translate into influenza symptoms \cite{zaporozhan2010mechanisms}, mediated by the cryptochrome protein-family which has been shown to be geomagnetic receptors \cite{rodgers2009chemical,engels2014anthropogenic,hiscock2016quantum}.

Whatever the cause, global biosphere disturbance theory represents an alternative paradigm compared to the traditional contact-transmissible contagion theory in the context of disease spreading dynamics. Our goal with this study is to present a novel mathematical bridge between these two paradigms which at its core is a connection between the Gompertzian growth model and the logistic growth model. Our proposed bridge represents an microscopic version to the well-known parametrization given by Richard (1959) \cite{richards1959flexible}, where he augmented standard logistical growth by one parameter. This is why it is also sometimes called $\theta$-logistic growth \cite{petroni2020logistic}.
% \begin{equation}
% \frac{d}{dt}\ln{Y(t)} = -\beta\left[1 - \left(\frac{Y(t)}{{Y_{\infty}}}\right)^\theta\right],
% \end{equation}
% where traditional logistic growth is augmented by a parameter $\theta \in \mathbb R^+$.

 One issue with Richard's parametrization is that the traditional communicable disease models cannot be realistically extrapolated to the Gompertz model, as shown by Wang et \textit{al.} (2012) \cite{wang2012richards}. The reason why Richard's model cannot realistically represent a communicable disease with a Gompertz model is because it would imply that there is ``long-range interaction'' between the susceptible and infected (see \cite{petroni2020logistic} for details), which is counter to the basic assumption that communicable diseases spread through proximity between the susceptible and infected. Based on these findings, one would then be forced to conclude that Gompertz growth cannot arise in the context of spread through proximity between the susceptible and infected.

In this paper we will show this incompatibility in another way by creating a microscopic model where each entity is exposed to an environmental stressor simultaneously applied to the population. This theory does not preclude the possibility of multiple sources of disturbance, potentially local and interacting with a global source. The main idea is that over some possibly geographically constrained area, everyone is exposed to the same source of disturbance simultaneously.

The organization of the paper is as follows: We first review the basic communicable disease models, before presenting a new stochastic model from which Gompertz growth emerges and thus explains the observed mortality data. We then proceed to juxtapose of the logistic model and the Gompertz model in the microscopic domain where multiple entities comprise the macroscopic behavior. The final section is dedicated to unifying the communicable disease paradigm with a biosphere disturbance though a new microscopic form of the Logistic-Gompertzian growth equations.

\section*{Simple communicable disease models: The SIR model family}
We start by reviewing the basic infectious disease model, namely the Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) model under a time-dependent infection/recovery rate ratio, $\phi(t)$ \cite{kermack1927contribution}, and show under what conditions the Gompertz curve could emerge. First assume a pool of susceptible people of size $N$ evolving between the three states: susceptible, $S(t)$, recovered, $R(t)$ and infected, $I(t)$,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:SIR}
\frac{dS}{dt}& = -\beta \frac{IS}{N}\nonumber\\
\frac{dI}{dt}& = \beta \frac{IS}{N} - \alpha I.\\
\frac{dR}{dt}& = \alpha I\nonumber,
\end{align}
omitting the argument $t$ in each variable for brevity and where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in this context signify recovery and infection rates.

A line of reasoning employed by Rypdal and Rypdal (2020) \cite{Rypdal2020} to obtain the Gompertz curve is to linearize the SIR model by assuming
both the number of infected, $I(t)$, and cumulative infected, $Y(t)$, are much less than the initial pool of susceptible people, $N \gg Y \ge I$, which seems well founded at the national level, viz.
\begin{align}
\label{eq:linSIR}
\frac{dY}{dt}& = \beta I\\
\frac{dI}{dt}& = (\beta - \alpha) I = \alpha (\phi(t) - 1) I,
\end{align}
and where the number of recovered, $R(t)$, is under this linearization decoupled from the other variables. They further assume the number of deceased is proportional to the number of cumulative infected, offset by a time lag, which allows us to use the same set of linearized equations to model the number of cumulative people deceased without loss of generality. And since $I$ is a function of $Y$, we can combine Eq. \ref{eq:linSIR} via an instantaneous relative growth rate, $\gamma(t) = dY(t) /(Ydt) = \beta I / Y$, 
\begin{equation}
\frac{dY}{dt} = \gamma(t) Y(t) \label{eq:rypdalODE}.
\end{equation}
Due to this linearization, the infection/recovery rate ratio, $\phi(t)$, and the relative growth rate, $\gamma(t)$, will have to change as a function of time to accommodate for the boundary conditions. The latter is typically also parameterized by a scaling factor, $\theta$, representing the shape of the growth,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rypdalGamma}
\gamma(t) = \frac{\beta}{\theta}\left[1 - \left(\frac{Y}{Y_{\infty}}\right)^{\theta} \right].
\end{equation}
This parametrization is the commonly used Richards growth curve \cite{richards1959flexible}, also called $\theta$-logistic growth, and has been used by others \cite{wu2020generalized}. Although not immediately justified in the communicable disease theory, one could imagine that $\theta$ represents non-linear network behavior \cite{petroni2020logistic}. Note that at $\theta=1$, the traditional logistic growth curve is obtained, while at $\theta\rightarrow \infty$ we recover the exponential (Malthusian) explosion.

The observed Gompertzian mortality curves are realized in the limit $\theta \rightarrow 0$, with the relative growth rate, 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rypdalLimit}
\gamma(t) = \lim_{\theta \rightarrow 0}\frac{\beta}{\theta}\left[1 - \left(\frac{Y}{Y_{\infty}}\right)^{\theta} \right]
= \beta\ln{\frac{Y_{\infty}}{Y}},
\end{equation}
such that 
\begin{equation}
\frac{d \ln Y}{dt} = -\beta \ln \frac{Y}{Y_\infty} \label{eq:gompertzODEfirst}.
\end{equation}

 The Gompertzian limit implies an exponentially decreasing relative growth rate from the very first time point, which under the SIR model is unlikely given the large pool of susceptible people in the beginning. 
 One would rather expect a near-constant relative growth rate in the beginning due to a disease propagation stage. 
 Rypdal and Rypdal (2020) \cite{Rypdal2020} suggest that the decreasing relative growth rate is caused by social and political mitigating efforts, but these hardly justify such coherent and consistent mortality characteristics across countries, particularly with an exponentially decreasing growth rate. In fact, Wang et al. (2012) \cite{wang2012richards} showed that any values of $\theta<1$ are not physically realistic under a similar SIR model as the one given above. 
 
 Perhaps a more likely scenario from which a Gompertz curve would emerge is the selective infection of central nodes in the transmission network resulting in an immediate decrease in relative growth. 
 Herrmann and Schwartz (2020) \cite{herrmann2020covid} studied a networked SIR model on a variety of networks, but did not elaborate on a possible fit to a Gompertz curve. Estrada and Bartesaghi (2022) \cite{estrada2022networked} found that the Gompertz curve can emerge in later stages of an SIS (Susceptible-Infected-Susceptible) model, but not in the beginning of an SIR-like model.
 Although it may be possible to realize Gompertzian growth from networks, firm theoretical work has yet to be done to establish this connection. 
 We will touch upon how the Gompertz curve emerges from such networks below.

 In general, the SIR models and their extensions which do not consider network interactions explicitly consider rather averaged macroscopic behavior of an ensemble of microscopic units justified through mean-field theory \cite{smilkov2014beyond}. In such models, all entities are implicitly assumed connected and communicating through long-range interactions as shown by Mombach et al. (2002) \cite{mombach2002mean}.
However, even with such strong assumptions, it is odd that observed mortality never exhibits an early stage of near-exponential growth as this macroscopic SIR model family predicts, but rather a constant negative slope in the double-log-domain, which we will now show. 

\subsection*{The extended SIR model family is almost Gompertzian}
Without the linearization and the $\theta$-parametrization of the Richards model, one can still almost obtain Gompertzian growth from the communicable disease SIR-like models. Under a double-log-transform of Gompertzian growth, the growth curve is a simple straight line which is one of its characteristic traits \cite{Levitt2020},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:GOMP_D}
\ln{(\ln{(Y_{\infty}/Y)})} = -\beta t + k,
\end{equation}
with $k=\ln{\ln{\frac{Y_{\infty}}{Y_{0}}}}$. To show this discrepancy between the SIR-like models and the Gompertz model, we follow Carletti et al. (2020) \cite{carletti2020covid} and consider the extended version of the SIR model by including a group of deceased, $D(t)$, such that $N = S(t) + I(t) + R(t) + D(t)$, also called the SIRD model. This extension requires an addition to our original set of equations \ref{eq:SIR}, with an equation for the deceased group's growth at some rate $\delta$ relative to the current infected group,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dD}{dt} = \delta I.
\end{equation}

Algebraic manipulations reveal that the deceased group is described by a single equation, viz.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:SIRD_D}
\frac{dD}{dt} = \eta( 1 - e^{-\xi D}) - \kappa D,
\end{equation}
where $\eta = \delta N$, $\xi = \beta/(\delta N)$, $\kappa = \delta + \alpha$, and where $N$ is again synonymous to the initial susceptible pool of people. This pool of people cannot be obtained from deaths alone, but can be inferred by assuming a known ratio between mortality and recovery rates, $\delta$ and $\alpha$. 
However, when only modeling the deceased as we do here, knowledge of $N$ is irrelevant and the three parameters in Eq. \ref{eq:SIRD_D} are sufficient.

In the beginning of an epidemic one can assume small values of D and thus the SIRD model follows logistic growth with
\begin{equation}
\frac{dD}{dt} = c D (1 - D/K),
\end{equation}
obtained with a second order expansion of the exponential term in Eq. \ref{eq:SIRD_D} and with $c=\eta\xi - \kappa$ and $K=2c/\eta\xi^2$. Both the SIR and SEIR model exhibit similar properties and their discrepancy with initial observations related to the Covid pandemic has been noted by others \cite{vattay2020forecasting}.

Without this approximation, the full 3-parameters in Eq. \ref{eq:SIRD_D} of the deceased group can show a curve quite close to a straight line in the double-log-domain even in the initial observations, although there will always be a non-zero concavity (Fig. \ref{fig:Gompertz_vs_SIRD_lnln_infty} and the SI Appendix). This concavity is a property of the model rather than a discriminating measurement: over a single wave it is small enough that both models track the observations closely, and as we show below the two are separated far more sharply by how well their parameters can be determined than by how closely they fit.

Meanwhile, the Gompertz model is estimated through linear regression of log-mortality \cite{Levitt2020} with only two parameters,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:GompertzODE2param}
\frac{d}{dt}\ln{Y(t)} = -\beta\left[\ln{Y(t)} - \ln {Y_{\infty}}\right],
\end{equation}
a rate $\beta$ at which the relative growth rate decays and an asymptote $Y_{\infty}$.
Its solution is closed-form,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gomp_solution}
Y(t) = Y_\infty \left(\frac{Y_0}{Y_\infty}\right)^{e^{-\beta t}},
\end{equation}
where $Y_{0}=Y(t = 0)$.

With two parameters the Gompertz model is parsimonious, and thus avoids the
non-identifiability that plagues more complex models \cite{roda2020difficult}.

In summary, we see that the SIR model family both lacks the observed immediate decrease in relative growth rate, and makes strong assumptions about the disease spreading network structure that imply non-local interactions. We are thus prompted to look for another model which can explain the observed mortality patterns. For this challenge, we shall first present a stochastic approach without a network and then present an approach with a network which unifies the SIR family models with the Gompertz curve.

\subsection*{The third SIRD parameter is not supported by the mortality data}
\label{sec:conditioning}

Both models describe the observed $(\dot{D}, D)$ relationship well, and judged by the
criterion on which they were fitted the SIRD is marginally the better of the two: across
the sixteen countries of Fig. \ref{fig:Gompertz_vs_SIRD} the median coefficient of
determination is $0.968$ for Eq. \ref{eq:SIRD_D} against $0.957$ for
Eq. \ref{eq:GompertzODE2param}, and the Akaike criterion prefers the SIRD in eleven of
the sixteen countries even after charging it for its extra parameter. We therefore do not
claim that these data reject the SIRD model, and we would caution against resting such a
claim on a formal test of one model against the other. Both curves lie close to the
observations over a single wave; a test that must distinguish them is being asked to
resolve a difference of the same order as the noise, and the verdicts it returns are
correspondingly unstable.

The more informative question is not which curve passes closer to the data but which
parameters the data are able to determine at all. A convenient diagnostic is the
condition number of the fitted Jacobian with each column scaled by its own parameter,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cond}
\mathrm{cond} = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(J\Theta)}{\sigma_{\min}(J\Theta)},
\qquad J_{ij} = \frac{\partial \dot{D}_i}{\partial\theta_j},
\qquad \Theta = \mathrm{diag}(\theta),
\end{equation}
which measures how sharply \emph{relative} changes in the different parameters are
distinguished by the data, and which is invariant both to the units of the individual
parameters and to any rescaling of the death counts. Table \ref{tab:stability} reports it
for both models. For Eq. \ref{eq:GompertzODE2param} it lies between $3.8$ and $4.4$ for
every country except China, and barely varies from one epidemic to the next: the rate and
the asymptote are close to orthogonal directions, whatever the data. For
Eq. \ref{eq:SIRD_D} it runs from $18$ to $594$, an order of magnitude larger and strongly
dependent on which country is supplied. The SIRD model is not unidentifiable --- its Jacobian has
full rank --- but the third direction is weakly determined, and it is precisely the direction that carries $\mathcal{R}_0$.

The structure of that weakness is transparent in the rescaled coordinates of Carletti
et al. \cite{carletti2020covid}. Writing $x=\kappa D/\eta$ and measuring time in units of
$1/\kappa$, Eq. \ref{eq:SIRD_D} collapses onto the one-parameter family
$dx/d(\kappa t) = 1 - e^{-\mathcal{R}_0 x} - x$: of the three parameters, $\eta$ sets the
vertical scale, $\eta/\kappa$ the horizontal scale --- the implied final death toll ---
and $\mathcal{R}_0$ alone the shape. The two scales can be read almost directly off the
observed peak height and the observed total, and are determined accordingly. What the
conditioning analysis reports is that the shape is not.

The practical consequence is a sensitivity to choices that ought to be immaterial. The
smoothing window applied to $\dot{D}$ is even in length, so it cannot be centred exactly;
placing it half a day to one side rather than the other changes no modelling assumption
whatsoever. Yet it moves the fitted $\mathcal{R}_0$ by a median of $15\%$ and by as much
as $18.6\%$ (Table \ref{tab:stability}), while the Gompertz rate $\beta$ moves by a
median of $1.4\%$ and the asymptote $Y_{\infty}$ by $0.8\%$. A quantity that shifts by a
sixth under a half-day realignment of a moving average is not being measured to the
three significant figures at which it is commonly reported.

The same instability is visible between independent implementations. Re-fitting
Eq. \ref{eq:SIRD_D} to the same source data as Carletti et al. \cite{carletti2020covid},
we recover their published $\gamma/\beta$ --- the ratio that fixes the implied final
death toll --- with a median ratio of $0.99$, within $5\%$ for eleven of sixteen
countries, and their $\gamma$ and $\beta$ individually to within about $20\%$, the two
moving together. But our $\xi$ is systematically some $25\%$ smaller, and since
$\mathcal{R}_0 = (\gamma/\beta)\,\xi$ the discrepancy passes undiluted into the
reproduction number, which comes out a median $24\%$ below the published values. Half of
that gap closes on adopting the odd-length window above. The point is not that either set of numbers is wrong, but
that two careful fits of one model to one data set can differ by a third in the quantity
the model is used to report, while the quantity the data actually constrain agrees to
one per cent.

Finally, the models are fitted in the $(\dot{D}, D)$ plane but read in time. These are
not the same test. Integrating each fitted equation forward and comparing with the
observed trajectory under the transform $g(Y)=\ln\ln(Y_{\infty}/Y)$ of
Fig. \ref{fig:Gompertz_vs_SIRD_lnln_infty}, the ordering reverses: the Gompertz residual
is smaller in eleven of sixteen countries, by a median factor of $2.1$ and by as much as
a factor of $10$ (Germany) and $8$ (Austria). The SIRD's slender advantage in the phase
plane is systematic rather than random --- it is the concavity noted above --- and
systematic error accumulates on integration, whereas the Gompertz residual, though
larger, does not. A model may fit the instantaneous relationship better and still track
the epidemic worse.

Taken together these observations suggest a different reading of the SIR family's
reputation for simplicity. Eq. \ref{eq:SIRD_D} is simple in form, and it is derived from
a transparent mechanism; but as an estimation problem posed against mortality data alone
it carries one parameter more than the observations can support, and the resulting
looseness is inherited by every quantity computed from the fit. Gompertzian growth, with
one parameter fewer, gives up a little of the fit and returns estimates that are stable
under the arbitrary choices of the analysis pipeline.

\section*{A stochastic theory for observed mortality}
\label{seq:alt}
An alternative line of reasoning that does not rely on the framework of communicable diseases is that the environment was perturbed by an instantaneous external stressor, initiating a stress response to eventually bring mortality rates back to stability.

Inspired by De Lauro et al. (2014) \cite{de2014stochastic}, the stressor can be modeled as a multiplicative stochastic dampening term along with a countering force of the immune system. 
As with all multiplicative processes, it is convenient to log-transform mortality and work in a dimensionless space such that $F(t)=Y(t)/Y_\infty$ and $Z(t)=\ln{F}(t)$, from which a natural perturbation model emerges,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:microscopic}
dZ(t)= -\beta Z(t) dt + \sqrt{\sigma}dB(t),
\end{equation}
where $dB(t)$ is a delta-correlated Wiener process with zero mean, making $Z(t)$ a stochastic process. The second term on the right hand side represents the ongoing perturbation due to the stressor, while the first represents the stress response.  

This perturbation model is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process \cite{risken1996fokker}, where $Z(t)$ is Gaussian. It is also interpreted as Newton's equation of motion with friction and a random force (Langevin's equation), and a continuous version of an Auto-Regressive(1) model \cite{akaike1970statistical}. As argued by De Lauro et al. (2014) \cite{de2014stochastic}, the diffusion coefficient, $\sigma$, represents the strength of the perturbation. This is seen if we recast our perturbation model in terms of mortality while introducing a new parameter $C=\exp{(-\frac{\sigma}{2\beta})}$, viz. 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:microscopicOriginal}
dF(t) = \left\{\frac{\sigma}{2}F(t) - \beta F(t)\ln\left[\frac{F(t)}{C}\right]\right\}dt + \sqrt{\sigma}F(t)dB(t).
\end{equation}

To obtain the deterministic observable, we first take the average in the log-domain (Eq. \ref{eq:microscopic}), 
\begin{equation}
d \;\mathbb E[\ln{F(t)}] = -\beta \mathbb E[\ln{F(t)}] dt,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb E$ signifies the averaging operation.
Then we use the property that for the log-normal quantity $F(t)$, the average of its logarithm is the logarithm of the \emph{median} quantity \cite{petroni2020gompertz}, where we denote the median of $F(t)$ as $M(t)$, 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:medianGomp}
d\ln{M(t)} = -\beta \ln{M(t)} dt,
\end{equation}
which is the deterministic Gompertz equation of Eq. \ref{eq:GompertzODE2param} in the normalised variable, with $Y(t) = Y_\infty M(t)$ and asymptote $M_\infty = 1$. 
The two terms of Eq. \ref{eq:microscopicOriginal} separate the roles cleanly: the positive drift $\sigma F/2$ is generated by the stressor, so its strength $\sigma$ is the source of growth, while $\beta$ is the growth-limiting factor. It is the constant $C=\exp(-\sigma/2\beta)$ that places the asymptote exactly at $M_\infty=1$, so the two are not independent, and neither can be read off a fitted mortality curve, on which only $\beta$ and $Y_{\infty}$ appear. This comparison is clear after taking the median of Eq. \ref{eq:microscopicOriginal} wherein the cross-term of $F(t)dB(t)$ goes to zero due to the independence of $F(t)$ and $dB(t)$.

Thus, a more parsimonious interpretation of the observations not reliant on a transmission network is that mortality was caused by a perturbation of the biosphere, modeled as a random process, to which organisms gradually develop resistance at a geometric rate in the log-transformed domain \cite{boxenbaum2017hypotheses,neafsey1988gompertz}, which is the natural transformation for many processes in nature \cite{zhang1994log}. 
Under this model, the distribution of the abundance of $F(t)$ is log-normal, a result that can be obtained by directly solving the stochastic equation \ref{eq:microscopicOriginal} \cite{skiadas2010exact,petroni2020gompertz}, or from thermodynamic principles \cite{sitaram1984statistical,gunasekaran1982lon,chakrabarti1996non}. 
Intuitively, this is seen by noting that the solution to the perturbation model in the log-domain (Eq. \ref{eq:microscopic}) is Gaussian in the variable $Z(t)$, thus suggesting a log-normal distribution of $F(t)$.

\section*{Coherent growth as a mechanism for the observed Gompertz curve}

The stochastic account above is a single-entity model. It is natural to ask what
distinguishes logistic from Gompertzian dynamics once a population of interacting
entities is admitted, and the answer bears directly on the estimation problem of the
previous section. We summarise the relevant results here; the derivations, the
interpolating family and the supporting simulations are given in a companion
paper \cite{haugen2026micro}.

The distinguishing property is closure under aggregation. Splitting the system into $N$
microscopic abundances $x_i$ and writing $z_i=\ln x_i$, Gompertz dynamics are linear in
$z$ and therefore closed under the geometric mean: the microscopic and macroscopic
domains obey the same equation, exactly and at every stage of the epidemic. Logistic
dynamics with transmission, $\dot{x}_i = \beta(1-x_i)\sum_j w_{ij}x_j$, are not.
Averaging leaves a term $\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{i}\sum_j w_{ij}x_ix_j$ which equals
$\beta\bar{x}^2$ only if neighbouring states are uncorrelated or all nodes are perfectly
synchronised --- and neither is compatible with a communicable disease, since the first
denies the transmission mechanism and the second denies any heterogeneity in health
status. Macroscopic logistic growth is thus an approximation conditioned on a property
its own microscopic model violates, whereas macroscopic Gompertz growth is exact.

Embedding both in the Richards family through an interaction order $\theta$, which
recovers Gompertz as $\theta\to0$ and logistic-type dynamics at $\theta=1$, the Gompertz
limit is the unique member for which the coupling term contributes exactly zero to the
aggregate growth \cite{haugen2026micro}. The transmission network becomes dynamically
invisible in the aggregate: it still governs each individual trajectory, but it leaves no
trace in the population curve. This is what connects the mechanism to
Table \ref{tab:stability}. If the aggregate mortality of a coherent population is blind
to the network, then no property of that network --- $\mathcal{R}_0$ among them --- can be
recovered by fitting a curve to aggregate deaths, and an attempt to do so will produce
exactly what we observe: a parameter that is formally estimable, but so weakly determined
that a half-day shift in a moving average moves it by a sixth.

We stress that the empirical results of the previous section do not depend on this
mechanism. That $\mathcal{R}_0$ is poorly conditioned in Eq. \ref{eq:SIRD_D}, and that
the Gompertz rate is not, are statements about the fit that hold whatever one believes
about the underlying dynamics. The coherent-growth account is offered as an explanation
consistent with those facts, not as a premise on which they rest.

\section*{Conclusion}
We have argued that the SIR family's reputation for simplicity does not survive contact
with mortality data. Eq. \ref{eq:SIRD_D} is simple in form and transparent in derivation,
but as an estimation problem posed against deaths alone it carries one parameter more
than the observations support. Its fitted Jacobian is an order of magnitude worse
conditioned than that of the Gompertz model, and the ill-conditioned direction is
precisely the one carrying $\mathcal{R}_0$. The consequences are measurable: a half-day
change in how an even-length moving average is centred --- a choice with no modelling
content whatsoever --- moves the fitted reproduction number by a median of $15\%$, while
the Gompertz rate moves by $1.4\%$; and two careful fits of the same equation to the same
source data differ by a quarter in $\mathcal{R}_0$ while agreeing in the median to about
one per cent on the combination the data actually determine. Reproduction numbers obtained this way are
routinely reported to three significant figures with uncertainties an order of magnitude
smaller than these shifts.

We do not conclude from this that the SIRD model is false. Over a single wave it fits the
observed $(\dot{D},D)$ relationship marginally better than the Gompertz model does, and we
have been explicit that a formal test between the two is a weak instrument at these sample
sizes. The conclusion is narrower and, we think, more useful: the quantity these models are
most often fitted in order to report is not identifiable from aggregate mortality, and the
apparent precision with which it is quoted is an artefact of an error model that ignores
the smoothing applied to the data. The Gompertz model, with one parameter fewer, gives up
a little of the fit and returns a rate and an asymptote that are stable under the arbitrary
choices of the analysis pipeline --- and, when the fitted equations are integrated forward,
tracks the observed trajectory more closely in eleven of the sixteen countries examined.

The Gompertz curve also admits a mechanistic reading. It is the unique member of the
Richards family that is closed under aggregation, and in that limit the transmission
network contributes nothing to the aggregate growth \cite{haugen2026micro}. A population
whose collective mortality is blind to its own contact network is one from which no
transmission parameter can be recovered by curve-fitting, which is what the conditioning
analysis measures from the opposite direction. Read together with the observation that
relative growth rates decrease from the first reported death, without the propagation phase
a finite-speed transmission process requires, this favours an account in which the
population is perturbed simultaneously by a common external stressor rather than
sequentially by a communicable agent. Whether that account is correct is a separate
question from the estimation result, which stands on its own.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Estimating model parameters for Covid data}
The time-evolution of the SIRD and the Gompertz model are obtained with a Runge-Kutta algorithm, after first fitting parameters using non-linear least squares according to equations \ref{eq:GompertzODE2param} and \ref{eq:SIRD_D} in the text (see SI Appendix for details). 
Fitting is done using Python-Scipy's non-linear least squares algorithm with specified Jacobians and a 6-day windowed average of observed deaths, similar to the technique by Carletti et al. \cite{carletti2020covid}. 
Death counts are normalised by the toll observed at the end of the fitting window before
fitting, which leaves $\beta$ unchanged and expresses $Y_{\infty}$ as a multiple of the
observed total.

Identifiability is assessed with the scaled condition number of Eq. \ref{eq:cond}. The
column scaling is what makes the diagnostic comparable between models with parameters of
different physical dimension, and it renders the number invariant to the normalisation
above; as a check, the SIRD fit for Italy returns $18.1$ whether deaths are counted in
units of one or of one thousand. Sensitivity to the smoothing convention is measured by
repeating every fit with a 7-day window, which unlike the 6-day window admits an exact
centre, and recording the relative change in each estimated quantity.

We caution against reading the reported standard errors as a full account of the
uncertainty. They are obtained from the least-squares covariance under the assumption of
independent errors, whereas $\dot{D}$ is a centred moving average, so residuals a day
apart share five of their six constituent observations and are strongly correlated by
construction. The intervals so obtained are consequently far narrower than the spread
between equally defensible analyses of the same data: the $\mathcal{R}_0$ values of
Table \ref{tab:sird} and those of ref. \cite{carletti2020covid} differ by amounts that
are tens of times the uncertainty either analysis quotes. The comparisons in
Table \ref{tab:stability} are reported for this reason --- a perturbation study of the
analysis pipeline bounds the reproducibility of an estimate more honestly than its
nominal standard error does.
Observations are taken from the Github repository compiled by the Center for Systems Science and Engineering (CSSE) at Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, USA \cite{dong2020interactive}.
